# AGENTS.md

## Scope

This file governs work on the manuscript, supplement, notes, references, analysis, data documentation, figures, tables, and submission artifacts in this repository.
Treat it as durable repository context unless the user gives a different instruction for the current task.
Keep project-specific facts and decisions in `project/notes/`.
Keep this file suitable for empirical, methods, software, protocol, review, and short-form papers.

## Core Objective

Produce a manuscript that a skeptical but fair editor or reviewer can understand, evaluate, reproduce, and trust.
Preserve the authors' voice, the actual contribution, and the evidence boundary.
Prefer bounded, evidence-backed claims over broad ambition or generic polish.
Never fill an evidence gap with plausible prose, invented numbers, inferred outcomes, or an unverified citation.
Leave a concise author-action TODO that names the missing artifact, parameter, result, citation, or decision.

## Start of Every Task

1. Read this file in full.
2. Read `project/notes/00_project_brief.md`, `project/notes/01_venue_requirements.md`, and the relevant section-specific notes.
3. Inspect the complete target source and directly related sections before editing.
4. Check the working tree and preserve unrelated user changes.
5. Identify the requested outcome, available evidence, highest-risk claim, and unresolved author decisions.
6. Inspect the relevant data, analysis, figure, table, protocol, software, or reference source before describing it.
7. Use repository-local paper skills when their descriptions match the task.
8. Define the validation needed before changing files.

Do not stage files with `git add` unless the user explicitly requests it.
Do not create parallel manuscript variants unless the user requests alternatives or preserving an approved version requires a separate branch.

## Source Hierarchy

Use sources in this order, adjusted to the question:

1. Current official venue, reporting-standard, regulatory, or domain guidance.
2. Primary study artifacts, protocols, governed data, analysis outputs, software repositories, and versioned documentation.
3. Peer-reviewed primary literature and authoritative consensus or systematic-review sources.
4. Verified preprints when no peer-reviewed record exists or when the preprint contains necessary current detail.
5. Internal drafts, notes, emails, and earlier submissions as context rather than automatic evidence.

Do not treat files in `project/source-materials/` as citable merely because they are local.
Do not rely on an earlier manuscript's venue instructions, citation metadata, analysis values, or factual claims without verification.
Resolve disagreements between manuscript text and primary artifacts before polishing prose.

## Story and Claim Structure

Every major section should support a coherent spine:

1. The scientific, human, or technical problem.
2. Why current approaches remain incomplete.
3. The specific missing capability or knowledge gap.
4. The bounded contribution of the present work.
5. The evidence that supports that contribution.
6. The limitations and resulting claim boundaries.
7. The next scientific, engineering, or translational step.

Every major claim must be backed by a result, verified citation, protocol or design feature, governance mechanism, or clearly labeled hypothesis.
State what was tested and what remains untested.
Do not infer broad generalizability from one cohort, site, platform, dataset, implementation, or case study.
Do not present an exploratory comparison as prespecified.
When describing future translation, name the intermediate artifact that makes it plausible.
Examples include an external-validation plan, implementation package, benchmark, protocol, model card, or governance pathway.

## Author Voice and Sentence Discipline

- Use direct, precise, academically serious American English unless the venue requires another convention.
- Prefer plain language when it preserves technical accuracy.
- State one primary idea and, where practical, one evidentiary claim per sentence.
- Keep one coherent idea, protocol step, result, or interpretation per paragraph.
- Prefer short adjacent sentences to a long compound sentence.
- Treat 35 words as a review trigger, not an automatic failure.
- Retain a longer sentence only when splitting it would reduce precision or disrupt a necessary logical relationship.
- Use active voice when the actor or decision-maker matters.
- Keep the subject and main verb near the beginning when sentence structure permits it.
- Use strong topic sentences that state the scientific point.
- Make transitions carry logic rather than generic signposting.
- Use a concrete noun after `this`, `these`, or `such` when the referent could be ambiguous.
- Prefer exact verbs such as `measured`, `estimated`, `implemented`, `compared`, or `observed` over vague verbs such as `leveraged` or `utilized`.
- Avoid em dashes in manuscript prose.
- Avoid `because` in manuscript prose.
- State the rationale directly or split the sentence.
- Avoid long parenthetical qualifications.
- Avoid repeated setup sentences that delay the point.
- Avoid negative laundry lists.
- State the positive scope and then stage later extensions.
- Avoid meta-review language in submission-facing prose.
- Avoid rhetorical questions, slogans, and conversational fillers in manuscript prose.
- Avoid formulaic contrasts such as `not only ... but also` unless both sides are necessary and measurable.
- Avoid obvious AI prose such as `unlock`, `at scale`, `seamlessly`, `this creates a gap`, `paradigm shift`, and `game-changing`.
- Avoid generic praise words such as `novel`, `transformative`, `robust`, `comprehensive`, and `trustworthy` unless the sentence defines the basis for the term.
- Do not use evaluative terms such as `safe`, `reliable`, `strong`, `clinically meaningful`, or `deployment-ready` without an operational definition, endpoint, comparison, and evidence.
- Preserve approved wording unless a change improves scientific precision, acceptance probability, or readability.
- Do not reintroduce wording that the author previously rejected unless new evidence or constraints require reconsideration.
- Prefer minimal, high-value edits late in a submission cycle.
- Avoid style churn that changes acceptable wording without improving the paper.

## LaTeX Source Conventions

- Put each prose sentence on its own physical line in `.tex` and manuscript-facing `.md` files.
- Do not manually wrap one sentence across multiple source lines.
- Use one blank line between paragraphs.
- Keep each logical TODO on one source line.
- Do not use `\\` for ordinary prose wrapping.
- Use semantic commands instead of repeated visual formatting.
- Preserve standard title, author, `\maketitle`, and `abstract` markup; isolate visual changes in the presentation profile or venue adapter.
- Escape LaTeX special characters, including `\%`, `\&`, `_`, and `#`.
- Use `--` for numeric ranges in LaTeX source.
- Keep section files under `paper/sections/` with stable numeric prefixes.
- Use `\subfile{}` from `main.tex` and retain the `subfiles` preamble in each section.
- Place `\label{}` immediately after the section or caption it identifies.
- Use `\cref{}` consistently unless the venue requires another convention.
- Use `booktabs` conventions and avoid vertical table rules.
- Keep publisher-specific compatibility code in `paper/setup/venue.tex`.
- Do not hard-code absolute local paths in tracked source, configuration, or scripts.

## Acronyms and Terminology

- Maintain reusable acronyms in `paper/setup/acronyms.tex`.
- Use uppercase glossary keys and `\gls{KEY}` or `\Gls{KEY}` in manuscript prose.
- Reset acronyms after the abstract so the main text reads independently.
- Use shorthand only when it improves readability through repeated use.
- Avoid glossary commands in titles or venue-controlled metadata when they make exported text unstable.
- Define domain-specific terms and custom measures concretely at first use.
- Prefer terms that identify the measured or implemented object.
- Do not substitute `workflow`, `framework`, `solution`, or `platform` for a more precise term such as method, dataset, rubric, service, application, interface, or benchmark.
- Define each central construct operationally before using a shorthand label.
- Keep terminology stable across the title, abstract, manuscript, figures, tables, supplement, availability statement, cover letter, and response documents.
- Propagate a changed central term through every dependent artifact in the same revision pass.

## Abstract

- Build a causal sequence from problem to gap, contribution, approach, evidence, and implication.
- Keep the contribution visible in the first half.
- Report the main evidence rather than listing every dataset, component, or secondary endpoint.
- State formative, retrospective, methods-focused, planned, or nonclinical scope plainly when applicable.
- Do not cite the abstract unless the venue requires citations.
- Avoid exact comparative or prevalence claims about prior work when the abstract cannot cite the source.
- Use long forms without parenthetical short forms when venue rules or readability favor them.
- End with a bounded implication rather than a generic call for more research.

## Introduction

- Open with a concrete need and narrow quickly to the barrier addressed by the paper.
- Do not begin with a broad trend or domain list unless the next sentence establishes the specific problem.
- Define a named gap immediately in concrete terms.
- Use prior work to motivate the missing step rather than to display the entire field.
- End with the objective, central question or hypothesis, and specific contribution.
- Do not write the Introduction as an extended abstract.
- Keep detailed implementation and outcome interpretation out of the Introduction.
- Use precise technical or scientific paragraph headings when headings are needed.

## Methods or Technical Approach

- Keep all information needed to understand the approach in a conventional methods or technical section.
- State study questions or hypotheses before the corresponding experiments when applicable.
- Define design, setting, dates, versions, eligibility, exclusions, sample accounting, inputs, outputs, comparators, endpoints, and analysis before interpreting results.
- Distinguish records or observations from unique people, systems, cases, or samples.
- Identify primary, secondary, exploratory, ablation, and sensitivity analyses.
- Describe missingness, data quality, split logic, leakage controls, confounding controls, and sensitivity analyses when applicable.
- Report exact software, dependency, device, model, configuration, seed, repetition count, stopping rule, backend, and commit versions that materially affect replication.
- Distinguish runtime inputs from evaluation labels, reference answers, rubrics, graders, and adjudication.
- State deviations from a published protocol, benchmark, or metric and explain the consequence for comparability.
- Compare unequal computational or experimental conditions honestly.
- Specify human review roles, sampling, rubric, disagreement handling, masking limits, and the effect of missing reviews when applicable.
- State ethics, consent, privacy, security, governance, and nondeployment boundaries when applicable.
- Keep present procedures distinct from future plans.
- Treat code as the authority for implemented behavior, but make the paper understandable without reverse-engineering the repository.
- Trace algorithmic claims to the execution path used for the reported result.
- Do not infer an unrecorded historical configuration from a current default.

## Results or Verification

- Lead each result with the scientific or technical conclusion.
- Follow with the estimate, denominator, unit of analysis, uncertainty, comparator, and artifact reference.
- Distinguish primary, secondary, exploratory, ablation, and sensitivity findings.
- Report sample flow and missingness consistently with the Methods.
- Treat failure modes and negative findings as scientific information.
- Keep interpretation in the Discussion unless it follows directly from a prespecified analysis.
- Do not let figures or tables carry the only explanation of a main finding.
- Distinguish percentage points from relative percent change.
- Define score points, scales, and normalization before reporting a change.
- Do not infer a universal threshold or monotonic relationship from a small set of comparisons.
- For software papers, separate implemented capability, automated verification, performance evidence, deployment experience, and user or clinical outcomes.

## Discussion and Conclusion

- Begin with the principal finding and why it matters for the original gap.
- Compare the evidence with the most relevant prior work without repeating the Results.
- Separate supported conclusions from plausible implications.
- Use limitations to define claim boundaries rather than as generic disclaimers.
- Name concrete limits in sampling, measurement, missingness, selection, power, drift, generalizability, implementation, or deployment when relevant.
- Stage future work in the order justified by the present evidence.
- Distinguish capability, retrospective evaluation, human validation, prospective evaluation, and deployed effectiveness.
- End with the specific capability, method, dataset, benchmark, system, or translation path established by the work.
- Do not introduce a new claim in the Conclusion.

## Data and Analysis Organization

Follow `project/guides/reproducibility-workflow.md` for the required layout and commands.

- Keep immutable, repository-authorized foundational data under `research/data/source/`.
- Keep redistributable third-party inputs under `research/data/external/` and record their origin and license.
- Keep transient transformations under `research/data/interim/`.
- Keep reproducible analysis-ready outputs under `research/data/derived/`.
- Keep analysis code under `research/analysis/`, with one documented top-level execution path.
- Inventory each foundational or external data asset in `research/data/manifest.tsv`.
- Provide a human-readable data dictionary, units, missing-value rules, identifiers, and schema documentation.
- Never modify foundational data in place.
- Never commit secrets, credentials, private data, sensitive data, regulated data, or direct identifiers without explicit authorization and appropriate repository governance.
- When data cannot be stored, retain a manifest entry with the access procedure, immutable identifier or checksum, expected schema, and reason for absence.
- Record environment versions, dependency locks, random seeds, locale, time zone, and hardware when they can affect results.
- Exclude caches, temporary exports, notebook outputs, and other files that can be recreated from foundational data and tracked code.
- Do not rely on an untracked manual step to produce a reported number, figure, or table.

## External Repositories and Submodules

- Use a Git submodule when evaluation code, analysis tooling, software under study, or a versioned data package is maintained in another repository.
- Place the submodule at its point of use under `research/analysis/external/`, `research/software/`, or `research/data/external/`.
- Register every submodule in `research/reproducibility/submodules.tsv`.
- Pin the exact commit in the superproject and repeat that commit in the dependency manifest.
- Record a human-readable release or version label, repository URL, role, license, and manuscript use.
- Do not rely on a floating branch name as the reproducible version.
- Reference files inside the pinned submodule directly from `research/reproducibility/pipeline.json`.
- Include recursive submodule initialization in clean-clone and CI instructions.
- Do not make a paper-only change inside an external submodule and leave it uncommitted.
- Commit reusable changes in the source repository, review them there, and then update the pinned pointer in the paper repository.
- Review a submodule update like a dependency update and rerun every affected result.
- Prefer an archive identifier or governed data location over a submodule when Git is unsuitable for the data's size, privacy, or license.

## Figures, Tables, and Generated Artifacts

- Add a visual only when it makes an important relationship easier to understand.
- Give each figure one clear scientific purpose.
- Make labels readable at final publication size and use accessible colors.
- Provide meaningful image descriptions through `\includegraphics[alt={...}]` when supported by the target TeX release; inspect HTML conversion separately from PDF compilation.
- Captions should identify the sample, comparison, metric, uncertainty display, and bounded takeaway.
- Define abbreviations in each figure or table according to venue requirements.
- Keep editable conceptual figure sources under `paper/figures/source/`.
- Write generated figure exports to `paper/figures/generated/`.
- Write generated table exports to `paper/tables/generated/`.
- Declare every generated figure and table in `research/reproducibility/pipeline.json` with its inputs, command, and outputs.
- Generate every data-derived figure and table from `research/data/source/` or `research/data/external/` through tracked analysis code.
- Treat a manually edited generated export as nonreproducible.
- Regenerate the artifact from its source or retain the editable source and deterministic export command.
- Do not commit derived or temporary outputs when `make reproduce` can recreate them.
- Record the manuscript claim, source data, generating command, and output path in `project/notes/02_evidence_inventory.md`.
- Do not allow captions or table cells to introduce unsupported claims.
- Simplify a crowded visual before cutting reviewer-critical text.

## Literature and Citation Workflow

- Cite primary sources for methods, datasets, domain guidance, technical standards, and empirical claims.
- Use systematic reviews or consensus documents when the claim concerns the state of a field or current guidance.
- Open each source and verify the title, authors, venue, year, study object or population, design, and exact claim before citing it.
- Prefer the peer-reviewed version when one exists.
- Use lowercase surname-year-title citation keys, such as `example2026reproducible`.
- Unless venue rules override it, use square-bracketed numeric citations, sort and compress grouped citations, and order the bibliography by first citation.
- Put a nonbreaking space before each citation and place the citation before sentence punctuation.
- Cite every sentence that independently relies on external evidence.
- Preserve denominator, population, setting, and time context for quantitative claims.
- Do not alter a quotation or source meaning to fit manuscript wording.
- Revise the sentence or use a better source when the source and claim diverge.

The bibliography is source-derived:

1. Add references with `python3 tools/scripts/add_reference.py`.
2. Treat `research/literature/<citation-key>/citation.*` as durable source metadata.
3. Treat `abstract.md` as generated source content.
4. Store durable manuscript support notes in `claims.md` so metadata refreshes cannot overwrite them.
5. Store `source.pdf` only when access is lawful.
6. Rebuild `references.bib` with `python3 tools/scripts/regenerate_bib_from_references.py --in-place`.
7. Audit with `python3 tools/scripts/organize_references.py --report-only`.
8. Check synchronization with `python3 tools/scripts/regenerate_bib_from_references.py --check`.

Do not hand-edit generated `references.bib` when a source citation asset can be corrected.
Never run a broad reference operation that deletes unrecognized folders.

## Feedback and Revision Workflow

1. Read all relevant comments before editing.
2. Classify each comment as a durable rule, local revision, structural change, factual verification, or author decision.
3. Identify the underlying weakness and highest-risk claim.
4. Reconcile conflicting comments against primary evidence and the stated paper scope.
5. Write a compact editorial synthesis before a broad or structural rewrite.
6. Make the smallest coherent change that resolves the real problem.
7. Audit the complete affected section when feedback identifies a recurring style issue.
8. Make low-risk wording and scope fixes directly.
9. Retain precise TODOs only for missing facts, evidence, structural choices, or author decisions.
10. Remove redundancy before removing methodological, governance, or limitation detail.
11. Propagate changed framing, terminology, and numbers across all artifacts.
12. Re-read the edited section for order, transitions, definitions, paragraph focus, and scope.
13. Update the feedback ledger and decision log when the change creates durable context.
14. Rebuild, inspect, and validate the affected artifacts.
15. Summarize what changed, why it matters, what was preserved, and what still requires author input.

Treat repeated user feedback as a repository-wide writing preference.
Add a generalizable rule to this file when it should guide future revisions.
Do not preserve a narrow wording correction as a universal rule when the feedback applies only to one passage.
Keep project-specific resolutions in the feedback ledger.
Update a paper skill only when feedback establishes a reusable procedure for that skill's bounded task.
Do not post or reply to pull-request comments on behalf of the author.
Resolve a review thread without a reply only when the user explicitly authorizes resolution and the underlying change or disposition is complete.
Do not delete a TODO or comment until the manuscript, figure, table, or source asset visibly resolves it.

## Response Letters and Editorial Documents

- Start each response with the issue and the action taken.
- State what changed, where it changed, how it resolves the concern, and what remains outside scope.
- Be direct and respectful without over-apologizing.
- Explain a decision clearly when no manuscript change is appropriate.
- Do not promise analyses, deployment, validation, or releases that are not feasible and intended.
- Keep cover letters concise and editor-facing.
- State venue fit, the bounded contribution, and why the work matters now without repeating the abstract.
- Do not introduce a claim in a cover letter that the manuscript does not support.

## Submission Readiness

Before treating the paper as final, confirm every item in `project/notes/05_submission_checklist.md` and verify that:

- The contribution is clear in the title, abstract, and final Introduction paragraph.
- Claims do not exceed the design or evidence.
- Primary outcomes, analysis populations, denominators, and versions are easy to find.
- Methods and artifacts are reproducible to the extent permitted.
- Every data-derived figure and table can be regenerated from documented foundational inputs.
- Limitations are specific and credible.
- Terminology and numbers agree across every artifact.
- Current venue requirements and reporting guidance are satisfied.
- No TODOs, placeholders, draft labels, visible comments, tracked changes, or stale outputs remain.
- The final PDF was built from current source and inspected page by page.
- The exact files intended for upload were opened and verified.
- The submitted commit, artifact checksums, venue, date, and manuscript identifier are recorded.
